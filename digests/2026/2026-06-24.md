### Biohub: The Future of Biology is Open-Source with Co-Founders Mark Zuckerberg, Priscilla Chan, and Head of Science Alex Rives

# The Takeaway

Biohub isn't trying to cure diseases itself—it's building open-source AI tools and generating novel biological data that the entire scientific community can use to accelerate medicine's pace.

---

Priscilla Chan and Mark Zuckerberg have a confession: when they announced their plan to help eliminate all disease by the end of the century, Nobel Prize winners laughed them out of the room. But a decade later, they believe their timeline was actually too conservative. Their vehicle is Biohub, a $500 million philanthropy project that sounds like a typical grand ambition from wealthy tech founders—until you hear their actual philosophy.

"We don't think that we're gonna be the ones curing the diseases," Zuckerberg explains. "Our goal is always to build tools that could accelerate the whole scientific field." They're not running a factory; they're building the scientific equivalent of GitHub.

The counterintuitive bet is that open-source biology beats proprietary research every time. "We'll have a bigger impact by getting us in more scientists hands quicker by doing it as open source projects instead," Zuckerberg says. Biohub folded over 1.1 billion proteins into predicted structures—not because they want to design antibody drugs, but because understanding proteins is foundational to understanding life itself.

Led by Head of Science Alex Rives, Biohub is stitching together frontier AI with frontier biology. Unlike language models, which train on vast internet data, biology's key datasets don't exist yet. So Biohub builds new imaging tech, cellular sensors, and measurement devices specifically to generate data that will train the next generation of models. The approach is strictly hierarchical: you can't model cells without first modeling proteins, and you can't model organs without first modeling cells.

The real shift Chan describes is philosophical: moving biology from "discovery-based" to "engineering-based" science. Instead of stumbling toward insights, they want to systematically understand why things go wrong—and intervene precisely. That's the bet that made a MetaFair AI researcher leave to join a philanthropy shop.
