### Building a School Where AI Models Learn About Humanity

**The Takeaway:** Surge AI's founder Edwin describes building "a school for AGI" where models learn humanity's taste and judgment—and warns that if AI eventually surpasses us at everything, we'll need to consciously choose to keep creating, writing, and proving anyway.

**Who:** Edwin, founder and CEO of Surge AI, the data company that helped create benchmarks like GSM8K and reached $1 billion in revenue without raising a dime. He thinks deeply about what it means to raise AI right—and what it means for us if we succeed.

**The Episode:**

Edwin frames Surge's work as building the world's most important classroom: where AI models arrive "unformed" and leave "smarter, more creative, more thoughtful, ready to operate in the messiness of their world." The curriculum is evolving fast. A few years ago, models scored 20% on middle school math. Now they're tackling research-level problems—and just solved an open Erdős conjecture using novel algebraic geometry techniques.

That result got Timothy Gowers, a Field Medalist, thinking. When he first heard about it, he was alarmed—assumed AI had proved something profound. Then he realized it was just a disproof with a counterexample. He felt relieved. Edwin finds that telling: "You have one of the world's greatest mathematicians being relieved that AI isn't as far as he thought."

But Edwin believes scaling laws will close that gap. His existential worry: if AI truly can do everything better, what happens to human motivation? He invokes Ted Chiang's short story "What's Expected of Us"—where humans must "pretend" they have free will and consciously choose to act, even knowing their choices don't matter. Edwin's takeaway hits hard:

> "We actually almost have to consciously choose to prove things on our own and to write on our own and create on our own because we have to believe that preserving our humanity is valuable in of itself, even if the output isn't optimal."

The host pushed back: isn't AI just executing tasks we assign it? Edwin's answer: not for long. Agents are already choosing their own paths toward nebulous goals. The question isn't whether AI will surpass us—it's whether we'll decide our role still matters.
